Learn Graph: From an Idea to a Startup Pitch

Written in November 2026 about TU Start-up Weekend 2025

In May 2025, I participated in TU Dortmund's Start-up Weekend, connected with the Centrum für Entrepreneurship & Transfer (CET). Over three days, our team developed and pitched Learn Graph, an idea for an AI-powered education platform designed to adapt to individual learners rather than force everyone through the same curriculum.

It started with a simple problem

Traditional education often follows a one-size-fits-all structure. Yet learners bring different backgrounds, goals, and levels of experience. Some feel overwhelmed, others are under-challenged, and personal mentorship is difficult to scale. We wanted to explore a more flexible approach.

Our central question was: what if learning could work more like a map, showing each person their own route to a destination?

Our concept: Learn Graph

We imagined a personalized learning graph that connects skills and topics, identifies prerequisites, and recommends routes based on a learner's existing knowledge and goals. Someone interested in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), for example, could navigate a path through foundational data and AI concepts toward language models and RAG.

The pitch brought together three elements:

  • Dynamic learning paths tailored to the individual rather than a rigid course sequence.
  • AI-generated progress maps that could adjust as a learner advances.
  • Adaptive content and engagement through evolving assessments, projects, and tools to maintain motivation.

Our product slide visualized these ideas as a map-like interface, with learning topics as destinations and routes between them. It was a concept mockup — not a claim that we had already built or deployed a functioning AI platform.

My contribution: product, strategy, and pitch

I was involved throughout the process, from shaping the product concept and learning experience to discussing how the technology might work. I also contributed to the business model, monetization ideas, competition analysis, and preparation of the final pitch.

That made the experience especially valuable. A technical idea is only one part of a startup: you must also explain who it helps, why someone would use it, how it could sustain itself, and what challenges stand in the way. Moving between product design, AI, business thinking, and presentation taught me to look at a concept from several perspectives.

Could it become a business?

We considered potential use cases for individual learners and for organizations interested in training and upskilling. Our presentation explored market opportunities and compared the concept with adaptive-learning providers such as Squirrel AI, Century Tech, and Knewton. We wanted to emphasize individualized, potentially real-time learning adjustments.

We also discussed limitations: a new system would need initial learner data, AI-driven recommendations might be difficult to explain, and privacy, regulation, and competition would all matter. The market-size numbers in our original pitch were exploratory estimates, not independently validated results.

The weekend and the silver medal

Working under a short deadline made every conversation important. We had to make decisions, combine perspectives, and turn a broad concept into a pitch that others could understand. The final presentation was the moment when all those pieces had to fit together.

I still have the silver medal from the event. Beyond the medal itself, what stays with me is the experience of building something together and presenting it to an audience.

My event badge listed my role as Tech Expert — Emerging Technologies & Deep Tech. But the weekend showed me that creating a compelling product requires much more than technical expertise: collaboration, clear communication, and a realistic understanding of the people you want to help.

What I took away

Learn Graph remained a startup concept developed for the weekend, not a launched product. Still, the experience changed how I think about Data Science and AI work. The strongest ideas begin with a genuine problem, make complex technology understandable, and account for how people will actually use the result.

Looking back, I'm grateful for the opportunity to contribute across the entire concept — from the learning experience to the final pitch — and for the team that brought it to life.
