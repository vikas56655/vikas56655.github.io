SRV ToneLab: Exploring the Science Behind the Blues

19 November 2026 · Python · Next.js · FastAPI · Signal processing

Stevie Ray Vaughan is one of my favorite guitarists. I wanted to turn that passion into a Data Science project: an interactive space for exploring what happens when blues guitar meets audio analysis.

Why I started

I wanted to build something personal rather than another generic machine-learning showcase. SRV ToneLab is an unofficial, open-source laboratory inspired by the expressiveness of blues guitar. The idea is to make aspects of sound visible, measurable, and interactive — while remembering that numbers alone cannot capture the feeling of a performance.

What the prototype does today

The current version, v0.2, is an experimental research preview. It includes several modules:

  • Audio Laboratory: Analyze a short WAV or an original synthetic demo using a waveform, FFT spectrum, RMS, spectral centroid, rolloff, and approximate pitch.
  • Guitar Intelligence: Explore time-resolved estimated pitch, a log-frequency spectrogram, spectral-flux onset candidates, and an interactive time scrubber.
  • Tone Explorer: Adjust drive, brightness, and sustain on a browser-generated guitar-like pluck to hear how audio processing changes the sound.
  • Discography and influence map: Explore an introductory selection of recordings and illustrative blues connections, with stronger source verification planned.
A research caveat: The pitch and onset methods are exploratory baselines. Bend and vibrato classifiers have not been validated, and the synthesizer is not a reproduction of SRV's exact tone.

How I built it

The frontend uses Next.js and React for navigation and interactive visualization. A Python FastAPI backend performs audio analysis, exposed through a same-origin API proxy. The project also includes API tests and a GitHub Actions workflow.

A spectrum shows how energy is distributed across frequencies, while the experimental pitch timeline estimates a predominant note over time. Those techniques become substantially harder with real-world guitar recordings, where harmonics, noise, bends, and simultaneous sounds interact.

The next research questions

The next milestone is to gather licensed, labeled examples of guitar notes, bends, and vibrato, then evaluate pitch tracking quantitatively. Beyond that, I want to explore more reliable music metadata, audio-embedding similarity search, and stronger production safeguards.

SRV ToneLab is still evolving. For me, the interesting part is using sound to ask better questions — and using data to investigate them without losing sight of the music.

Follow the project

The source code and roadmap are public. The demo audio is original and synthetic; no copyrighted SRV recordings are bundled.
